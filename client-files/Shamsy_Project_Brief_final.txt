 Project brief
 Shamsy all-in-one business platform · confidential · for shortlisted candidates only




 1 · What this business actually does
 Shamsy imports solar equipment and sells it in Sudan. Inverters, batteries, solar air
 conditioners, and from this year solar panels, balance-of-system parts and solar water pumps.
 We buy from suppliers in Saudi Arabia, China and the UAE. We do not install anything. We sell
 to roughly 570 dealers and installers who do the installing, and to a smaller number of large end
 customers.

 A shipment arrives in Port Sudan, clears customs, and is trucked to our warehouse in
 Omdurman. From there it goes out in ones and twos to dealers across the country. Our latest
 battery and inverter container was over $220,000 of goods at purchase price; a container of
 panels is 720 panels, around $93,000 in revenue.

 The war destroyed most of the national grid. Generating capacity fell from 4,400 MW to about
 1,100 MW, and blackouts run to eighteen hours a day. Solar is how people keep a freezer
 running and a phone charged. Demand is not our problem. Cash, currency and coordination are.



 2 · How the money moves — read this before anything else
 The whole system exists because Sudan's banking does not work normally.

 Euros → dollars → stock → Sudanese pounds → euros. The owner starts with euros.
 Suppliers want dollars, so he exchanges euros for dollars and pays a commission on top.
 Worked back, that tells him what each dollar of stock cost him in euros: the cost factor.

 On the August 2026 shipment he received 1.1674 dollars per euro and paid €211 commission.
 So every dollar of goods cost him €0.860234. Check: $59,983 of goods × 0.860234 = €51,599,
 almost exactly the €51,593 he put into that shipment.

 Each shipment gets its own factor, and once set it never changes: the euros were spent on the
 day of the exchange, so a later move in the euro–dollar rate does not change what that stock
 cost. When a supplier is paid with dollars already held — for instance from sales received on the
 UAE account — the factor is what those dollars cost. The system multiplies dollar cost by the
 shipment's factor to get cost in euros, which is how net profit in euros is calculated.

 The stock is sold in Sudan in Sudanese pounds. Customers pay directly into bank accounts
 held by money exchangers. The exchangers convert the pounds and pay the owner out in
 euros, at their rate of that day.

 Every result in both currencies, as an amount and as a margin. The owner must be able
 to see each line below in dollars and in euros, side by side or with one switch, and each as an
 amount and as a percentage of revenue:

 In dollars, costs are the landed cost in dollars. In euros, costs are converted through the
 shipment's cost factor, and revenue through the rate at which the pounds were actually
 converted — or, while still unconverted, the rate at which they were booked.

 The result depends on where and how much is converted. Pounds can be converted by
 an exchanger in Sudan, who pays out in euros; or paid by customers into the UAE account,
 where they arrive as dirham or dollars and are converted through Airwallex — or not converted
 at all, because dollars on the UAE account can pay the next supplier directly. With the pound
 falling fast, more and more payments are expected to arrive in the UAE. So the system shows
 the currency result per route — each exchanger, and the UAE account — with the rate
 achieved, the volume converted, and what it earned or cost against the pricing rate. Money still
 held in dollars or dirham is shown as held, at the rate it came in at, not counted as euro profit
 until it is actually converted.

 Prices are set in dollars. Each morning the day's rate is multiplied in, so what we charge in
 pounds never lags the currency. The pound falls almost daily: in June 2026 we priced at around
 6,050 SDG per dollar; by September it is above 8,000. So the rate is never a constant in code. It
 is a setting the owner changes, often every day, and below it sits a minimum rate, also set by
 the owner, under which no adviser can quote.

 We also price on a conservative calculation rate and convert at whatever the exchangers give
 that day. The difference is the currency result: on the August shipment it was worth $4,135 —
 gross margin 25.7%, net margin 30.3%.

 The system works in four currencies: US dollars (prices, purchasing, margin), Sudanese
 pounds (what customers pay), euros (what the owner starts and ends with) and UAE dirham
 (payments received through the UAE account). Every amount carries its currency and the rate
 at which it was converted.

 name or a sequence like "Ihab 1" to "Ihab 6", which is how a split order is reconstructed.

 The owner then has to forward each proof to the exchanger so the exchanger knows that
 money is his. He has caught himself forgetting. Money that was received but never forwarded
 sits with an exchanger who does not know it belongs to us.



 3 · Who works in this system
 The owner, in Europe. Buys, arranges shipping, negotiates supplier terms, manages the
 currency chain, sets the daily rate, approves large discounts and price overrides, and gives the
 team weekly direction. Works in English and Dutch.

 The head of marketing, Amira, in the UAE. Must not see cost prices or margins. Runs
 Facebook (14,000 followers), TikTok (4,000) and the dealer WhatsApp groups (570 dealers).
 Also receives dirham payments. Announces shipments before they land, so she needs to know
 what is in stock and what is arriving.

 Two sales advisers, Sana in Dongola and Marwa in Khartoum. Take orders, quote, chase
 payments, forward proofs. Work on phones, often on a bad connection. Their current sales tool
 is in Arabic; the new system starts in English, with Arabic added later. They must never see cost
 prices or margins.

 The warehouse, Jawahir in Omdurman. Receives shipments, releases goods against paid
 orders, counts stock. Sees no financial data. Once took a cash payment, so cash receipts by
 staff must be possible.

 4 · What already exists, and what it teaches
 This is not a greenfield project. Four working tools already encode how the business runs. Read
 them before designing anything.

 The sales tool — Shamsy_Verkooptool_v7.html, version 7. A single offline HTML file the
 advisers open on their phones, sent around as a WhatsApp attachment. Arabic, right-to-left. The
 adviser enters the day's rate, picks products from a fixed list, sets quantities, and builds a
 multi-line quote with totals in dollars and Sudanese pounds. Its rules are deliberate and must
 survive into the new system:

 • Advisers cannot change prices. Dollar prices are fixed. They choose product, quantity and
   rate. The point is that nothing can go wrong in the calculation.
 • Cost prices and margins never appear. An adviser sometimes hands her phone to a
   customer.
 • A minimum rate, enforced three ways: the field turns red with a message, the add button
   refuses, and on leaving the field the value snaps back to the minimum. A higher rate is
   always allowed. In the current file the minimum is hard-coded — at 8,000 since version 7 —
   which is exactly what must not happen in the new system: the pound falls almost daily, from
   around 6,050 per dollar in June to above 8,000 in September, so the minimum has to be a
   setting the owner changes himself.
 • Only SDG and USD. Saudi riyal was deliberately removed. With USD the rate field is
   disabled and set to 1.
 • Works offline, large buttons for thumbs, numbers formatted with thousands separators.

 Planned for it and still open: customer name and date on the quote so it can be shared as a
 receipt; editing quantity in an existing line; changing the minimum rate behind an owner-only
 code; a share or print button; and possibly a second price column for pick-up in Saudi Arabia,
 without transport and clearing costs.

 The daily administration — Shamsy_Daily_Admin_v10.xlsx. Thirteen sheets: Read me,
 Customers, one sheet per adviser, Warehouse, Forwarding check, Rates, Cash and bank,
 Dashboard, Result, Products, Accounts, Checks. Order numbers carry a prefix per adviser, SA-
 and MA-. Each order gets two payment lines that fill in its order number automatically. A
 generated WhatsApp message tells the customer the amount, the account, and how many
 transfers of 3,000,000 it takes. Payment methods: BANKAK, UAE Account, Cash. Discounts are
 coloured by size, and above 5% the sheet refuses the entry unless the owner has marked the
 order as approved.

 The shipment dossier — Shamsy_Zending_Growatt_v24.xlsx. Every cost of a shipment —
 invoice, sea freight, bank charges, customs clearing in several payments in SDG at their own
 rates, inland transport — spread over the products pro rata to purchase value, with a 20%
 safety uplift on indirect costs. On the current shipment that lifts the purchase price by 12.44%:
 an inverter bought at $270 has a true cost of $303.58. Also tracks margin per order and the
 supplier payment deadline.

 The cash register — Shamsy_Kasregister. The event-based ledger built from 211 payment
 screenshots: one line per unique transaction code, from which account to which account,
 amount, comment, and whether it appears in the sales sheet. Plus a sheet of open questions
 where nothing is booked until the owner answers.

 5 · What a day looks like now, and why it hurts
 A dealer sends a WhatsApp message: two 5 kWh batteries and an inverter. An adviser opens
 the sales tool, enters the rate, builds the quote, and sends a screenshot of it. She writes the
 order in the daily spreadsheet.

 The dealer pays in two or three instalments, each capped at 3 million pounds, into accounts
 that may be full. He sends a screenshot of each transfer. The adviser forwards them to the
 owner. The owner forwards them to the exchangers. Someone matches them to orders by hand,
 in the evening, sometimes days later.

 The warehouse releases goods on a WhatsApp message that sometimes arrives before the
 money does.

 At the end of a shipment, 64 payment proofs are reconciled against 22 orders across three
 currencies. The earlier ledger was kept one tab per exchanger, which meant one event had to
 be written in several places; the result was that the summary sheet missed 38 transactions
 worth 103,803,000 SDG and the exchanger tabs missed 8 worth 20,570,000. Excel read
 decimal numbers as dates. Account holders' names were spelled five ways.

 What goes wrong, repeatedly:

 • Payments cannot be matched to orders without reading a screenshot
 • Proofs are not always forwarded to the exchanger
 • Nobody knows how much room is left in an account today
 • Stock leaves against a verbal confirmation
 • Margin per shipment is known weeks after the goods have gone
 • Nobody can prove the books balance



 6 · Principles the system must hold to
 These come from the owner's own rules for his administration. They are not preferences.

 • History must never move. The pound falls almost daily, so rates change constantly. Every
   order line, receipt and conversion is stored with its amount, its currency and the rate of the
   day it happened, and that rate is never recalculated from a newer one. An order placed at
   8,000 SDG per dollar still shows 8,000 in six months when the rate may be 11,000. A report
   on any past period must give exactly the same numbers whenever it is run, to the last pound.
   Money is stored as integers in the smallest unit, never as floating-point numbers.
 • Book per event, with accounts as balances. Every money movement is one line with an
   origin and a destination. Customer to Umhani is one line; Umhani to an exchanger is a
   second; exchanger to the owner's euro account is a third. Never book a movement under its
   final destination and skip the intermediate step, or the intermediary becomes uncheckable.
 • The transaction code is unique. The same code can appear only once. Duplicate
   screenshots are harmless because of it.
 • Same code, different amount: do not choose. Show both to the owner.

Shamsy                                                                                              5
 • When in doubt, book nothing. An unknown account goes on a question list. Nothing is
   booked on it until the owner answers.
 • Show contradictions, never smooth them over. A visible gap is better than a number
   that looks right.
 • Never invent an amount. Mark every assumption.

 9 · Where this is going, so build for it
 Phase two turns the same system into a product our dealers use: their quotes, their customers,
 their stock, their bookkeeping, at a price that is easy to say yes to.

 A dealer who runs his business inside our system orders from us. His quotes are built on our
 catalogue and our prices. And through it we see what the market is quoting and selling, and
 what our dealers still hold, weeks before it reaches us as an order. They get a system; we get
 the market.

 So the data model is multi-tenant from day one, and every permission lives in the database.
 The platform is also white-label and configurable: each environment has its own branding,
 and every business rule that is specific to Shamsy — discount thresholds, minimum rate,
 currencies, account types — is a setting, not code. That keeps the door open to reselling it and
 to data-driven revenue beyond our own trade. Do not build the dealer screens. Do build a
 structure that will not need rewriting for them.

 12 · Going live
 The system does not start empty. Before the team switches over, the developer imports from
 our spreadsheets:

 • the opening balance of every account, as confirmed by the owner on the switch-over date

 15 · Paid trial task
 What this is. Before we award the project, the shortlisted candidates each build one small,
 real piece of it: the screen where a sales adviser records an order. It takes six to eight hours
 and we pay $100 for it, whether or not we continue with you. It shows us how you think about
 money, rules and phones — which is what the whole project depends on.

 The situation you are building for. A dealer in Khartoum asks our sales adviser on
 WhatsApp for four inverters, two small batteries and one large battery. The adviser opens this
 screen on her phone, picks the dealer, adds the products, gives a small discount where she is
 allowed to, enters today's exchange rate, and sees what the dealer has to pay in US dollars and
 in Sudanese pounds. Then she saves the order.

 The rules the screen must enforce

 1. Prices are fixed. Each product has a price in US dollars. The adviser cannot change it.
 2. Discount is per line, and separate from everything else. The adviser may give a
   discount in dollars on any line. The discount is measured as a percentage of that line's value
   (price × quantity):
    – above 0% and up to 3% — the line turns sand, and saving is allowed
    – above 3% and up to 5% — the line turns red, and saving is still allowed
    – above 5% — saving is blocked until the owner approves that line
 3. The exchange rate is one number for the whole order. It converts dollars to pounds. It
   has nothing to do with discounts or prices. It can never be lower than the minimum rate of
   8,000 SDG per dollar. If the adviser types a lower number, the screen refuses it and puts
   the value back to 8,000. A higher number is always fine.
 4. A saved order never changes. The rate the adviser entered is stored on the order. If the
   rate setting changes tomorrow, yesterday's order still shows exactly the same amounts.
